Building a discounted cash flow analysis, start to finish

A DCF values a company, division, or set of assets as the present value of the free cash flow it is expected to produce. Because it rests on assumptions rather than market prices, it is the standard cross-check on comps and precedents — and it is only as good as those assumptions. This guide walks the five steps bankers use to build one, with the checklist, formulas, and numbers from the ValueCo model built into each.

Study the target and determine key performance drivers

Shortcuts here distort every assumption that follows

Learn as much as possible about the target and its sector before touching a model: business model, financial profile, value proposition, end markets, competitors, and key risks. Then isolate the drivers of sales growth, profitability, and FCF generation — they are what let you build, or defend, a set of projections. This is far easier for a public company than a private one, because the filings and research already exist.

Checklist

Where drivers come from

Internal
  • Opening new facilities or stores
  • Developing new products
  • Securing new customer contracts
  • Improving operating and working capital efficiency
External
  • Acquisitions
  • End-market trends
  • Consumer buying patterns
  • Macro factors, legislative or regulatory change

Project free cash flow

The core of the DCF, and the input with the greatest effect on value

Unlevered FCF is the cash a company generates after all cash operating expenses and taxes, and after funding capex and working capital, but before any interest. Because it is independent of capital structure, it represents cash available to every capital provider — debt and equity.

Calculate weighted average cost of capital

The discount rate that reflects the target's business and financial risk

WACC is the weighted average of the required return on debt and equity at the target's capital structure. It works as an opportunity cost — what an investor would expect from another investment of similar risk. For a company with very different business segments, consider a sum-of-the-parts DCF with a separate WACC for each.

Checklist

The formulas

WACC = [ rd × (1 − t) × D/(D+E) ] + [ re × E/(D+E) ] re = rf + βL × (rm − rf) + SP risk-free + levered beta × market risk premium + size premium βU = βL ÷ [ 1 + (D/E) × (1 − t) ] βL = βU × [ 1 + (D/E) × (1 − t) ]

4

Determine terminal value

Often three-quarters of the answer, so it gets the most scrutiny

Projecting FCF forever is impossible, so a terminal value captures everything beyond the projection period. It can be as much as three-quarters or more of the DCF value, which is why the terminal-year data must be a normalized steady state, not a cyclical high or low. Two methods are widely accepted, and each checks the other.

Checklist

The formulas

Exit multiple method TV = EBITDA(n) × Exit multiple Perpetuity growth method TV = FCF(n) × (1 + g) ÷ (r − g) r = WACC, g = growth Implied growth rate (TV from exit multiple, mid-year) g = [ TV × WACC − FCF(n) × (1+WACC)^0.5 ] ÷ [ TV + FCF(n) × (1+WACC)^0.5 ] Implied exit multiple (TV from perpetuity growth, mid-year) x = TV × (1+WACC)^0.5 ÷ EBITDA(n)
End-of-year versions drop the (1+WACC)^0.5 terms: implied g = (TV × WACC − FCF) ÷ (TV + FCF); implied multiple = TV ÷ EBITDA.

Calculate present value and determine valuation

Discount, sum, bridge to equity, then test the range

Multiply each year's FCF and the terminal value by its discount factor, sum them to an enterprise value, and bridge to equity value. Because a DCF rests on so many assumptions, the output is a range built with sensitivity analysis — then compared with comps, precedents, and LBO analysis as a sanity check.

Checklist

The formulas

Discount factor (mid-year) = 1 ÷ (1 + WACC)^(n − 0.5) Discount factor (year-end) = 1 ÷ (1 + WACC)^n Enterprise value = Σ PV of FCF + PV of terminal value Equity value = EV − Debt − Preferred − NCI + Cash Implied share price = Equity value ÷ Fully diluted shares

Pros and cons

Pros
  • Cash flow-based — a more fundamental approach than multiples
  • Market independent — insulated from bubbles and distressed periods
  • Self-sufficient — works with few or no pure-play comparables
  • Flexible — run growth, margin, capex, and working capital scenarios
Cons
  • Depends on projections — harder as the period lengthens
  • Sensitive to assumptions — small changes move value meaningfully
  • Terminal value — up to three-quarters or more of value
  • Constant capital structure — no flexibility over the period
A gap versus other methods is a prompt, not a verdict. Unrealistic projections are the most common cause — especially when a Management Case is used without testing it. But multiples can also miss company-specific factors, so a difference does not by itself mean the DCF is wrong.
