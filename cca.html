Building a comparable companies analysis, start to finish

Trading comps value a company by looking at how the public market prices similar businesses today — turning a peer set's share prices into a defensible multiple range for the company in front of you. This guide walks the five stages bankers use to get there, with the checklist, formulas, and layout of a real working model (ValueCo / Gasparro Corp.) built into each one.

1

Select the universe of comparable companies

Study the target first — the peer list falls out of that story

Before you can find comparables, you have to know the target cold: its sector, products, customers, distribution, and geography, alongside size, profitability, growth, returns, and credit profile. Companies that share those traits trade on similar logic — so the peer list is really a byproduct of understanding the business, not a separate research task.

2

Locate the necessary financial information

Every multiple you calculate has to trace back to a filing or a consensus estimate

With the universe set, gather the historical and estimated financials each comparable's multiples will be built from. Historical statistics come from SEC filings; forward-looking figures come from consensus research estimates — and the two sources shouldn't be mixed within the same analysis.

Checklist

Where each input comes from

Income statement & estimates
  • Sales, gross profit, EBITDA, EBIT, net income — most recent 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, press release
  • Forward sales / EBITDA / EBIT / EPS — Bloomberg, Refinitiv IBES, S&P Capital IQ, Thomson First Call, or equity research
Balance sheet, cash flow & shares
  • Cash, debt, shareholders' equity — most recent 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, press release
  • D&A and capex — most recent 10-K, 10-Q, 8-K, press release
  • Basic shares outstanding — 10-K, 10-Q, or proxy, whichever is most recent
  • Options and warrants — 10-K or 10-Q, whichever is more recent
Market data
  • Share price, 52-week high/low, dividend — financial information service
  • Credit ratings — Moody's, S&P, Fitch websites
Don't mix sources mid-stream. Pick one consensus-estimate provider and stick with it across the whole comp set — switching providers between companies quietly changes what you're comparing.
3

Spread key statistics, ratios, and trading multiples

One input page per company — this is where the model does its work

Each comparable gets its own input page: market valuation and general company info on one side, the reported and adjusted income statement rebuilt to an LTM basis on the other, and a set of calculations that turn raw filings into equity value, enterprise value, margins, growth rates, return ratios, credit ratios, and trading multiples.

Checklist

The two formulas the page turns on

LTM period = Prior full year − Prior-year stub (same months) + Current-year stub (same months)
Enterprise value = Equity value + Total debt + Preferred stock + Noncontrolling interest − Cash & equivalents

4

Benchmark the comparable companies

Put every peer on one page and let the target's relative position show itself

With every comparable spread the same way, lay out their financial statistics and ratios alongside the target's in spreadsheet form. This benchmarking exercise determines the target's relative ranking, identifies its closest comparables, and flags the outliers — informing how the peer set gets tiered before you touch a single multiple.

5

Determine the valuation

Turn the benchmarked multiples into a range for the company you're actually valuing

The benchmarked multiples only matter once they're applied back to the target's own financials. Select the multiple(s) and range that best reflect the target's positioning within the peer set, apply them to the target's own metrics, and bridge the result back to an implied enterprise value, equity value, and — for a public target — a per-share price.

Checklist

From multiple to per-share value

Implied EV = Target LTM/forward metric × Selected multiple range Implied equity value = Implied EV − Debt − Pref. − NCI + Cash Implied per-share value = Implied equity value ÷ Diluted shares

Comps sets the floor, not the whole picture. Trading comps tends to sit tighter and lower than precedent transactions, since it carries no control premium — treat a wide gap between the two as a prompt to revisit assumptions, not as license to average them into one number.
