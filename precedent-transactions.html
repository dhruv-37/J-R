Building a precedent transaction analysis, start to finish

Precedent transactions value a company by looking at what acquirers actually paid for comparable targets in past M&A deals — turning a scattered set of past deal prices into a defensible multiple range for the company in front of you. This guide walks the five stages bankers use to get there, with the checklist, formulas, and layout of a real working model built into each one.

1

Select the universe of comparable transactions

Screen for the deals worth studying, before you study any of them

The output of this analysis is only as good as the deals you feed into it. Start broad, then apply screens that map to how a buyer would actually think about the target's business — industry, size, geography, and deal structure — until you're left with a working list you can defend line by line.

2

Locate the deal and financial information

Every number in the model needs a filing behind it

Once the universe is set, the work becomes research: chasing down the terms of each deal and the target's financials at the time it was announced. Public-target deals leave a paper trail through regulatory filings; private and sponsor deals rely more on press releases, sector reports, and disclosed estimates.

Checklist

Source documents, by target type

Public target
  • Merger proxy / scheme document — full deal terms, board rationale, projections
  • 8-K or RNS-style announcement — headline price and date
  • 10-K / 10-Q — LTM financials as of the announcement
Private or sponsor-backed target
  • Press release and sponsor/acquirer disclosures
  • Trade press, sector reports, and any disclosed post-close commentary
  • Comparable company data as a sanity check where financials aren't public
Watch for gaps. Private-target deals often disclose a headline multiple but not the underlying financials — flag those cells rather than guessing at a number that will quietly become a "fact" later in the model.
3

Spread the key data, ratios, and multiples

One input page per deal — this is where the model does its work

Each transaction gets its own input page: general deal terms on one side, the target's income statement rebuilt to a trailing-twelve-month (LTM) basis on the other, and a set of calculations that turn raw filings into the equity value, enterprise value, and multiples you'll compare across deals.

Checklist

The two formulas the page turns on

LTM period = Prior full year − Prior-year stub (same months) + Current-year stub (same months)
Enterprise value = Equity value + Total debt + Preferred stock + Minority interest − Cash & equivalents
A deal's headline price is rarely its equity value. Watch for collar structures on stock deals, earn-outs excluded from the up-front price, and assumed debt that changes the enterprise-value bridge.
4

Benchmark the comparable transactions

Put every deal on one page and let the pattern show itself

With every deal spread the same way, lay them all out on a single output page — same columns, same order — and compute the summary statistics that turn a list of one-off deals into a usable range.

Precedent multiples usually run above trading comps — they carry a control premium and any synergy value the buyer was willing to pay for. Don't be surprised when this range sits higher than your public comps range; that gap is doing its job.
5

Determine the valuation

Turn the range into a number for the company you're actually valuing

The benchmarked multiples are only useful once they're applied back to the target's own financials. Pick the metric the sector actually trades on, apply a defensible slice of the range, and bridge the result back to a per-share value you can place alongside every other method.

Checklist

From multiple to per-share value

Implied EV = Target LTM EBITDA × Selected multiple range Implied equity value = Implied EV − Debt − Pref. − NCI + Cash Implied per-share value = Implied equity value ÷ Diluted shares
A range, not a number. The point of the football field is to show where methods agree and where they diverge — resist the pull to average everything into one figure before you understand why the ranges sit where they do.
